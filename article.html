Ashfall Protocol turns a dead planet into the year’s best puzzle box

Forty hours in a collapsing terraforming colony, and not one of them wasted. After a decade of survival games that mistook tedium for tension, here is one that finally has something to say.

By Ada Ferreira 6 September 2026 12 min read

The first thing Ashfall Protocol takes away from you is the map. Not as a tutorial gimmick, and not for the first twenty minutes — permanently. The Meridian colony was built by people who never expected to leave it, and the game asks you to learn its corridors the way its dead engineers did: by walking them until the shape of the place lives somewhere behind your eyes.

That decision could have been insufferable. Plenty of games have mistaken inconvenience for immersion, and the survival genre in particular has spent ten years confusing a hunger bar with a reason to care. What saves this one is that every system it hands you is a sentence in an argument, and the argument turns out to be worth forty hours of your life.

A colony that answers back

Meridian is a terraforming station three years into a failure nobody has admitted yet. Ash falls constantly, in densities that shift with each reactor cycle, and the colony’s remaining automation reacts to it — sealing doors, rerouting power, venting corridors that were safe an hour ago. You are not exploring a level. You are negotiating with a building that has its own priorities.

The genius is in how legible those priorities are. Every closed door tells you something about where the power went. Every rerouted corridor is a clue about which system came back online, and why. Within six hours I stopped reading the colony as an obstacle course and started reading it as a diagnostic readout, which is precisely the moment the game wants you to reach.

Every closed door tells you something about where the power went.

What follows is the best stretch of environmental problem-solving since the genre’s early peaks. Reaching the eastern greenhouse is not a matter of finding a key. It is a matter of working out that the greenhouse only draws power when the reactor is in its cooling phase, that the cooling phase floods the access tunnel with ash, and that the only breathable window is roughly ninety seconds wide. Nobody tells you this. The colony simply behaves consistently until you notice.

Survival with a thesis

The survival mechanics themselves are almost conservative. You manage air, warmth, and a filtration supply that degrades with use. There is no crafting tree three hundred nodes deep, no base building, no cosmetics store waiting at the end of a progress bar. Sable Interactive has cut everything that does not serve the central question, which is what people owe each other when the plan has already failed.

That question is asked mostly through logs, and I want to be careful here, because audio logs are the most exhausted device in the medium. These work for one reason: they are unreliable. Two engineers describe the same evacuation vote, and their accounts do not match. The game never adjudicates. By the time you reach the archive and can check the vote record yourself, you have spent thirty hours forming an opinion about who was lying, and finding out is genuinely uncomfortable.

  • Air, warmth, and filtration are the only meters, and each one maps to a specific decision the colony’s designers made.
  • No crafting tree, no base building — scarcity comes from routing, not from grinding.
  • Logs contradict each other, and the archive lets you settle it only at the very end.

Where it stumbles

Two things keep this from being flawless. The combat, such as it is, exists mainly to interrupt. Three encounter types recur across the whole campaign, and each of them resolves into the same crouch-and-wait pattern within minutes. The game is noticeably better in the long stretches where nothing is hunting you, and it seems to half-know this — the final third almost abandons the idea.

The second problem is the middle. Hours eighteen through twenty-four sag badly, largely because the northern wing repeats spatial ideas the eastern wing already made better. I would happily trade that entire wing for two more hours of the finale.

The last six hours

And the finale is remarkable. I will not describe it beyond saying that the game finally gives you the map, and that receiving it feels less like a reward than an indictment. Everything you worked out on foot is confirmed on paper, including the parts you would rather have been wrong about.

Ashfall Protocol is not a comfortable game, and it is not a generous one. It is, however, the most confident thing anyone has built in this genre in years — a survival game that understands scarcity is only interesting when it forces a choice, and that a dead planet is only haunting if somebody used to live there.

9

Essential

A survival game with a real argument, held together by the best environmental design of the year. The combat is a tax you pay for the rest.

What works

  • A colony that behaves consistently enough to be read like a document
  • Systems cut back to only what the story needs
  • Contradictory logs that trust you to reach your own verdict
  • A finale that recontextualises forty hours of walking

What doesn’t

  • Three combat encounter types, stretched across a whole campaign
  • The northern wing repeats better ideas from earlier
  • Hours 18 to 24 sag noticeably
